GEOL 445 | Case 3 | Radioactive clocks

Reading an Isochron

Rocks that formed together start on one flat line. Radioactive decay tips that line up a little more every year. The tilt is the age, and the place the line pivots is where the rocks started.

1The problem an isochron solves

A mineral today contains some 87Sr. Part of it was there when the mineral grew. The rest was made since then, one 87Rb atom at a time (87Rb → 87Sr + β−, half-life about 49 billion years).

If we knew how much 87Sr the mineral started with, the age would be simple arithmetic. We don't. One sample gives one equation with two unknowns: the starting strontium and the time.

The way out is to measure several samples that started with the same strontium isotope ratio but different amounts of rubidium. Minerals crystallizing from one well-mixed magma do exactly that: the melt gives every crystal the same 87Sr/86Sr, but K-feldspar and biotite take in lots of Rb while plagioclase takes in almost none. Several samples, two shared unknowns: now it can be solved, and a straight line does the solving.

Why the minerals get different amounts of Rb

It comes down to ion size, the same Goldschmidt rules from Week 1. Rb+ (radius 148 pm) is close in size to K+ (133 pm), so it slips into K-feldspar and the micas; there are no Rb minerals of its own. Sr2+ (113 pm) substitutes for Ca2+ (99 pm) and prefers plagioclase, where its solid/melt partition coefficient is about 2 (White, 2023, §2.4.1, p. 57). One granite therefore hands out Rb very unevenly and Sr isotopes evenly: exactly what an isochron needs.

The same chemistry says where the method works. Crystallizing plagioclase pulls Sr out of a melt while Rb stays behind, so felsic rocks often have Rb/Sr above 1 (about 87Rb/86Sr = 2.9). The Earth as a whole has Rb/Sr of only 0.021–0.029, and basalts are low too, which is why Rb–Sr dating struggles on mafic rocks and does well on granites (White, p. 57).

Using several minerals from one rock, or a mineral plus its whole rock, this way goes back to Nicolaysen (1961) and Fairbairn and others (1961). Geochronologists still call it an internal isochron (Hodges, Treatise 4.8, p. 290). The name means "same time": a line whose slope depends only on time (White, §2.2.1, p. 41).

A way to picture it. Four jars are sealed at the same moment. Each already holds the same depth of blue sand in the bottom. Each also has a different amount of red sand in a top chamber, and every jar lets the same fraction of its red sand fall through per year. Later you measure red-on-top and blue-plus-fallen on the bottom. The jar with the most red on top has gained the most on the bottom. Plot top against bottom and the four jars fall on a line. The line's steepness tells how long they have been draining; where it meets the axis tells how much sand was in the bottom to begin with.

2Rewind a real rock: the Baltimore Gneiss

The Baltimore Gneiss is the old basement rock under Baltimore, Maryland, and one of the classic Rb–Sr datasets in textbooks. Wetherill, Davis and Lee-Hu (1968) measured five whole-rock samples and minerals separated from two of them. The numbers below are as White (2023) gives them in Chapter 2, Problem 9 (p. 86).

Each point is what the lab measured today. Drag the slider to run the decay backward: every point gives back the 87Sr its Rb made. Find the time when the points line up flat. That moment is when they last shared one 87Sr/86Sr, which is the age.

Three things to notice

Rewinding moves every point down and to the right. Each 87Sr made by decay was once an 87Rb. Both axes are divided by stable 86Sr, so a sample gains on the x-axis exactly what it loses on the y-axis. Run forward, the same samples climb up and to the left.

Samples with more Rb move farther. Rock 3 and the biotite travel farthest; rock 4 and the plagioclase barely move. Because each sample's change is proportional to its Rb, the points stay close to a straight line at every time. That is why the method works.

The line pivots on the y-axis. A sample with no Rb would never change, so the point at x = 0 stays at the starting ratio. The intercept is a fossil: it records the strontium these rocks had when the clock started.

The two buttons give two different flat moments, about 1.0 billion and about 0.3 billion years ago. Section 5 explains why both are right.

The Treatise draws the same picture run forward for uranium and lead: points migrate along arrows away from a shared start (Schoene, Treatise 4.10, Fig. 3b, p. 344).

3The equation is the picture

The isochron equation is the line from the figure, written out. Build it one step at a time:

  1. 87Srnow = 87Srstart + 87Srgrown inStrontium-87 is either original or made by decay. Nothing else adds it in a closed system.
  2. 87Srgrown in = 87Rbstart − 87RbnowEvery Rb atom that is gone became one Sr atom.
  3. 87Rbstart = 87Rbnow eλtDecay law, run backward. We measure Rb today, so write the start in terms of now.
  4. 87Srgrown in = 87Rbnow (eλt − 1)Substitute step 3 into step 2 and factor out Rbnow.
  5. Divide every term by 86Sr86Sr is stable, so it is the same number at the start and now. Note that x is the present-day 87Rb/86Sr, the ratio you measure, not the one the mineral grew with (White, p. 40). A mass spectrometer measures an isotope ratio far more precisely than it can count moles of either isotope, so dividing by 86Sr also removes the largest source of error (Schoene, Treatise 4.10, p. 344).
(87Sr/86Sr)now = (87Sr/86Sr)start + (87Rb/86Sr)now · (eλt − 1)
y  =  b  +  x · m
y and x: what you measure today. One point per sample. Every point is a pair of measured ratios.
b: what it started with. The same for every sample, so it is the intercept. Unknown until you draw the line.
m: the growth factor. The same for every sample because they share one age. It is the slope.

Once the slope is read off the graph, solve for time:

Why the slope is the clock, in one subtraction. Write the equation for two samples from the same rock. Both share the same starting ratio and the same t. Subtract one from the other and the starting ratio cancels:
Δ(87Sr/86Sr) = Δ(87Rb/86Sr) · (eλt − 1)
Rise over run between any two samples is eλt − 1. That is all "the slope" means (White, eqs. 2.18–2.19, p. 41).
t = ln(1 + m) / λ
Shortcut when m is small: ln(1 + m) ≈ m, so t ≈ m / λ (about 1% high at m = 0.02)

The shortcut is the first term of the Taylor series eλt = 1 + λt + (λt)2/2 + …, which is fine while λt ≪ 1 (White, eqs. 2.9–2.10, p. 40). For Rb, 1/λ is about 70 billion years, so even old granites qualify.

Try it first: fit the line by eye

Drag the two orange handles (or select one and use the arrow keys) until the line runs through the five rocks as well as you can. The dashed sticks show how far each rock sits from your line. Watch the age change as you tilt it.

Now the same thing with arithmetic:

  1. Slope: m = (y3 − y4)/(x3 − x4) = (0.7992 − 0.7074) / (6.59 − 0.2313) = 0.0918 / 6.3587 = 0.01444Rise over run, using the two rocks farthest apart in Rb/Sr. They pin the slope best.
  2. Age: t = ln(1 + m)/λ = ln(1.01444) / (1.42 × 10−11 yr−1) = 0.01433 / (1.42 × 10−11 yr−1) = 1.01 × 109 yrThe slope is eλt − 1, so add 1, take the natural log, divide by λ.
  3. Intercept: b = y4 − m·x4 = 0.7074 − 0.01444 × 0.2313 = 0.7041Any point on the line gives b once m is known.
  4. Check the other three rocks. Predicted y = 0.7041 + 0.01444·x: rock 1 0.7365 (measured 0.7380), rock 5 0.7564 (0.7573), rock 2 0.7566 (0.7612).All three sit slightly above the line, by 0.001 to 0.005. Close, but this is real data: no rock lands exactly on a line.

Answer: the whole rocks last shared one 87Sr/86Sr about 1.01 billion years ago, starting near 0.704. A best-fit line through all five rocks gives the same age, 1.01 Ga, with an intercept of 0.7054. Different pairs of points give slightly different intercepts, which is exactly why White says real ages come from many samples and a regression line, not two points (§2.2.1, p. 41). The shortcut t ≈ m/λ gives 1.02 Ga, about 1% high.

Your turn: date the minerals. From rock 3, biotite has 87Rb/86Sr = 289.7 and 87Sr/86Sr = 1.969; plagioclase has 0.528 and 0.7767. Calculate the age from these two minerals.
Answer

m = (1.969 − 0.7767)/(289.7 − 0.528) = 1.1923/289.17 = 0.004123. t = ln(1.004123) / (1.42 × 10−11 yr−1) = 2.90 × 108 yr = 290 Ma. Same rock, very different age from the whole rocks. Section 5 explains why.

Your turn: how sure is λ? The official decay constant is 1.42 × 10−11 yr−1, but recent measurements range from 1.421 × 10−11 (Rotenberg, 2005) down to 1.393 × 10−11 yr−1 (Nebel and others, 2011), as listed in White's Table 2.1 (p. 38). Recompute the whole-rock age with the lowest value.
Answer

t = ln(1.01444) / (1.393 × 10−11 yr−1) = 1.03 × 109 yr = 1.03 Ga, about 2% older than 1.01 Ga. The slope did not change; only our knowledge of the clock's rate did. That is why every published age states which λ it used, and why ages from the 1960s are recalculated with today's constants.

5Two lines on one plot

An isochron dates the last time its samples shared one 87Sr/86Sr. Which event that is depends on which samples you put on the plot. The Baltimore Gneiss shows both on one graph.

Whole-rock isochron (steep line, 1.01 Ga). Each point is a different rock from the same body. A later heating could move Sr between minerals inside one rock, but not between rocks collected far apart, so each whole rock kept its total Rb and Sr. The whole rocks still record the older event.

Why distance matters. Diffusion evens out isotopes over centimetres far more easily than over metres. Rocks collected metres to kilometres apart can only be re-homogenized by a hotter, longer event, or by a fluid (magma or hydrothermal water) carrying Sr between them. So metamorphism often resets minerals in a hand sample while leaving whole rocks hundreds of metres apart untouched (White, §2.2.1, p. 43, and §2.4, p. 56).

Mineral isochron (shallow line, 290 Ma). Each point is a mineral from rock 3 alone. A much younger event, during the Paleozoic building of the Appalachians, evened out their 87Sr/86Sr and restarted their clock. Its intercept (about 0.775) is rock 3's ratio at that time, not the original magma's 0.705.

Why the lines meet near rock 3. The whole rock is its minerals added together, so its point should sit on its own mineral line. Here it lands within 0.003 of it; again, real data.

Both ages are correct. They answer different questions: when did this body of rock form? and when did rock 3's minerals last trade strontium?

Each mineral is also a thermometer

A mineral does not need to be melted to restart its clock. While it is hot, Sr diffuses in and out and keeps trading with the minerals around it, so no radiogenic Sr builds up. As the rock cools, diffusion slows until the mineral effectively locks its Sr in. That temperature is its closure temperature (Dodson, 1973). Each mineral has its own.

Hodges lists bulk closure temperatures of about 500 °C for Rb–Sr in K-feldspar, 490 °C in muscovite and 320 °C in biotite, and about 960 °C for U–Pb in zircon. In the slowly cooled granite above, a K-feldspar isochron reads about 75 Ma and a biotite isochron about 57 Ma. Both are good dates. The biotite one is a cooling age: when the rock passed through about 320 °C, not when it crystallized. Zircon closes above the magma's temperature, so it records crystallization itself.

Back to the Baltimore Gneiss: rock 3's 290 Ma mineral age is set mostly by its biotite, which closes near 320 °C. So it most likely marks when the rock cooled below that temperature after its Paleozoic heating, a cooling age rather than the moment of heating itself. Hodges also notes that closure-temperature theory treats each mineral on its own, while an internal isochron assumes the minerals traded Sr with each other, so closure temperatures for mineral isochrons are approximate (pp. 290–291).

6A date is not yet an age

The number that comes out of t = ln(1 + m)/λ is a date. Hodges (Treatise 4.8, p. 285) calls it an age only when these hold:

Same start

All samples were in isotopic equilibrium when the clock started: crystals from one well-mixed melt, or minerals evened out by heating. Samples from unrelated rocks can line up by accident.

Closed since

No Rb or Sr gained or lost after that moment. Hodges calls this the most troublesome assumption. Rb and Sr dissolve in water, so fluids can carry them in or out, and micas give up Rb easily (White, p. 58).

Known starting daughter

For one sample this is a guess. The isochron removes the guess: the intercept measures it. That is the whole advantage of the method.

White (§2.4, p. 56) adds two practical conditions to the list:

  • Plenty of parent. Isotope ratios can be measured to a few parts per million, but if radiogenic 87Sr is only a few tens of ppm of the total, the date is not worth much. This is the basalt problem from section 1.
  • A wide range of parent/daughter. The uncertainty on the slope shrinks as the samples spread out along x. A wide spread also makes small differences in starting ratio matter less.

And the decay constant must be known, which is the point of the second "your turn" in section 4.

White sums up Rb–Sr's best uses: silicic igneous rocks that have not been altered or metamorphosed since, and metamorphic rocks themselves (p. 58).

Schoene (Treatise 4.10, p. 345) gives the same list for every isochron, in any decay system, and adds that it has to be checked two ways: statistically, and against the geology of the samples. The statistical check comes later in the course.

Then name the age

White puts the general rule in one line: the event an isochron dates is the last time the samples could fully exchange parent and daughter, which is usually the last time they were hot enough to do so (§2.2.1, p. 42). Hodges names the possibilities (p. 286):

NameThe samples last shared one ratio when…
Igneous agethey crystallized from a melt
Metamorphic agethe minerals recrystallized in the solid state
Cooling agethe mineral cooled through its closure temperature

The graph cannot tell these apart. The rocks can.

Race the clocks

Drag the age to see how much of each parent has decayed. Uranium-235 is nearly used up after 4.5 billion years, while Sm–Nd has barely started. That is why each clock has its own useful age range (section 8).

The most famous isochron dated the Earth. Clair Patterson (1956) put lead from meteorites and from Earth on one lead–lead isochron and got 4.55 ± 0.07 billion years: the time when meteorites and Earth began evolving separately (Schoene, Treatise 4.10, p. 345). On a Pb–Pb plot every sample starts at the same point and moves along its own path set by its U/Pb. The samples still fall on a line, and its slope gives the time without measuring uranium at all. The idea came from Arthur Holmes and Fritz Houtermans, working separately in 1946. One catch: the Pb–Pb slope cannot be solved for t with algebra, so it is found by guessing t, computing the slope, and adjusting until they match (White, eq. 2.16, p. 40).

A real Rb–Sr isochron

White's Figure 2.11 (p. 58) shows five analyses from a clast in the Bholghati meteorite (data of Nyquist and others, 1990) on an isochron of 4.54 Ga. Every x value is below 0.2, far less spread than the Baltimore whole rocks, yet the line works because the rock is so old: at 4.54 Ga the slope is eλt − 1 = 0.067, so the line still climbs about 0.013 across that small range. Open the figure and check that rise for yourself.

Isochron thinking at Earth's surface

The shared starting ratio does not have to come from a magma. Minerals that grow from seawater all inherit seawater's 87Sr/86Sr, so a Rb-rich clay (celadonite) and a Rb-poor, Sr-rich calcite from one sediment can be put on an isochron (White, §2.4.1, p. 59). Seawater can play that role because Sr stays in the ocean long enough to mix completely: a long residence time, the same box-model idea from Case 2. That uniform but slowly changing seawater ratio is also the basis of Sr isotope stratigraphy (§2.4.2, pp. 58–59).

8What geologists use today

The Baltimore Gneiss was dated in 1968, when whole-rock Rb–Sr isochrons were a workhorse. If a geologist dated a rock this year, the most likely tool would be U–Pb on zircon. Schoene calls zircon "by far the most commonly utilized mineral" for U–Pb dating (Treatise 4.10, p. 341). Zircon is common in granites and ash beds, survives weathering and transport, and closes near 960 °C. With two uranium clocks in one crystal it gives built-in cross-checks, and it works from 4.57 Ga into the Pleistocene (p. 341). Labs measure it three ways: dissolving single grains (ID-TIMS, the most precise), or spot by spot with an ion probe (SIMS) or a laser (LA-ICP-MS), which is fast enough to date hundreds of detrital grains from one sandstone (§4.10.4, pp. 352–355; detrital zircon, p. 367).

The rest of the modern toolkit, roughly by how often you will meet it in papers:

  • 40Ar/39Ar on sanidine, biotite or basalt groundmass, the standard for volcanic rocks and many fault and metamorphic micas.
  • Thermochronology: (U–Th)/He and fission tracks in apatite and zircon, which close at low temperatures and record when rocks neared the surface (Hodges, Treatise 4.8).
  • Young material: radiocarbon for organic carbon under about 50,000 years, and U-series (230Th/U) for carbonates like the Timpanogos stalagmites.
  • Surface processes: cosmogenic nuclides (10Be, 26Al) for how long a surface has been exposed or buried.
  • Special targets: Re–Os for molybdenite in ore deposits and organic-rich shales; Sm–Nd and Lu–Hf for garnet in metamorphic rocks.

Where Rb–Sr fits now. Whole-rock Rb–Sr isochrons are less common because Rb and Sr move with fluids. But Rb–Sr is back in a new form: a laser spot on a single mica, with a reaction cell in the mass spectrometer that separates 87Sr from 87Rb, so micas can be dated where they sit in a rock (Hogmalm and others, 2017). And the isochron idea runs through nearly all of these methods: Ar–Ar isochrons, Pb–Pb, garnet Sm–Nd and Lu–Hf, Re–Os. Learn it once here and you can read all of them.
